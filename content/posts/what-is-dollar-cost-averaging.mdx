## What Is Dollar-Cost Averaging?

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) is an investing method where you buy a fixed amount of an asset at regular intervals, such as $50 of Bitcoin every week, no matter what the price is. Because the amount stays the same, each purchase buys more when the price is low and less when it is high, so your average cost evens out over time instead of depending on a single entry point.

The idea is much older than crypto. Benjamin Graham described it in The Intelligent Investor in 1949 as a way for ordinary investors to buy stocks without guessing where the market was heading, and anyone paying into a workplace pension from every paycheck is already doing a version of it. Crypto just makes the case more vivid. On October 11, 2026, Bitcoin closed at about $83,800, roughly a third below its record close of about $124,700 on October 6, 2025. Anyone who put all their money in at that peak is still underwater. Someone who spread the same money over the following year is not.

This guide explains how the math works, runs real Bitcoin backtests from 2021 to 2026, covers the cases where DCA loses to investing everything at once, and walks through setting up a plan. If prices swinging 10 percent in a week still feel alarming, our guide to **[crypto volatility](/posts/crypto-volatility)** is a good companion read.

## How Dollar-Cost Averaging Works

A DCA plan has three settings: what you buy, how much you spend each time, and how often. Once those are fixed, the price stops being a decision. You buy on schedule whether the market is euphoric or in a panic.

The averaging effect comes from keeping the dollar amount constant rather than the number of coins. Take four monthly purchases of $100 while Bitcoin moves from $100,000 down to $80,000, then $50,000, then back up to $80,000:

- **Month 1 at $100,000**: $100 buys 0.00100 BTC
- **Month 2 at $80,000**: $100 buys 0.00125 BTC
- **Month 3 at $50,000**: $100 buys 0.00200 BTC
- **Month 4 at $80,000**: $100 buys 0.00125 BTC

You have spent $400 and own 0.0055 BTC, an average cost of about $72,700 per coin. The simple average of the four prices is $77,500. Your cost lands below it because the cheapest month bought the most coins. At $80,000 the position is worth $440, a 10 percent gain, even though the price is still 20 percent below where you started.

That is the whole trick. DCA does not predict anything. It simply makes sure the months when the market is on sale carry more weight in your average than the months when it is expensive.

## A Real Test: Buying Through the 2025-2026 Drop

Made-up prices only go so far, so we ran the same comparison on actual Bitcoin daily closing prices from Binance's BTC/USDT market. One investor puts $12,000 into Bitcoin on October 6, 2025, the day of the record close. A second investor spends the same $12,000 as $1,000 on the 6th of each month for 12 months, with the last purchase on September 6, 2026. Fees are left out to keep the comparison clean.

By the close on October 11, 2026, the lump-sum investor held about 0.096 BTC worth roughly $8,100, a loss of around 33 percent. The DCA investor held about 0.155 BTC at an average cost of about $77,200, worth roughly $13,000, a gain of about 8.5 percent. Same money, same coin, same year. The difference is that the second investor kept buying through the slide to Bitcoin's lowest close of the period, about $58,600 on June 30, 2026, and those cheap months pulled the average down.

A longer window tells a similar story. Someone who bought $50 of Bitcoin every week for five years, 260 purchases from October 24, 2021 to October 11, 2026, invested $13,000 in total. Their first buys came close to the November 2021 peak, and they kept going through the 2022 crash, when Bitcoin closed as low as about $15,800 on November 21, 2022. They ended with about 0.304 BTC at an average cost of roughly $42,700, worth about $25,500 at the October 11, 2026 close. That is nearly double what they put in, even though the price on that date was only about 38 percent above where they started.

## When Lump Sum Beats DCA

DCA is not free. Its cost is that money waiting to be invested sits on the sidelines, and markets rise more often than they fall. A 2023 Vanguard study of global stock market data from 1976 to 2022 found that investing a lump sum immediately beat spreading it out about two-thirds of the time.

Crypto shows the same pattern in rising markets. Run our test again from January 1, 2023, when Bitcoin closed around $16,600 in the aftermath of the FTX collapse. A $12,000 lump sum was worth about $30,500 by December 31, 2023. The same money split into 12 monthly purchases was worth about $18,900, because most of the buying happened after the price had already climbed. The lump-sum investor finished about $11,600 ahead.

So the honest framing is this: DCA tends to give up some return on average in exchange for protection from the worst outcome, which is putting everything in right before a crash. Many people find that trade worth making, especially in an asset that has fallen more than 70 percent at least four times since 2011. Our comparison of **[crypto trading vs holding](/posts/crypto-trading-vs-holding)** covers how these approaches fit different temperaments.

Two points keep the debate in proportion. First, it only applies to money you already have, such as a bonus or an inheritance. If you invest part of every paycheck, you are not choosing between lump sum and DCA at all, since the money arrives in installments anyway. Second, DCA cannot rescue a bad asset. Buying a fixed amount every week of a token that is slowly going to zero just averages you into a bigger loss, which is why DCA works best on established assets and not on hyped launches that may turn out to be a **[rug pull](/posts/what-is-a-rug-pull)**.

## Why DCA Suits Crypto in Particular

Crypto magnifies the problem DCA was built to solve. Prices can move 10 percent in a day, and the swings are driven as much by mood as by news. That makes timing the market extremely hard, and it makes the urge to buy after a rally and sell after a crash unusually strong.

A DCA schedule takes those decisions away from you. When the **[Fear & Greed Index](/posts/crypto-fear-and-greed-index-for-beginners)** sits at extreme fear and headlines are calling the end of crypto, the plan buys anyway, and that is usually when prices are lowest. When everyone is euphoric, your fixed amount buys fewer coins, which quietly limits how much you add near a top. The hard part is not the math. It is leaving the plan alone halfway through a bear market, which is exactly when it does its most useful work.

Some of the largest buyers in the market apply a version of the same idea. Strategy, the company co-founded by **[Michael Saylor](/crypto-ogs/michael-saylor)**, has been buying Bitcoin in repeated tranches since August 2020 rather than in one big trade. Its purchases vary in size and are funded by selling stock and debt, so it is not textbook DCA, but the principle of buying through the whole cycle is the same. According to its filing on October 12, 2026, Strategy held about 848,250 BTC at an average cost of roughly $75,400 per coin, below the market price that week despite having bought through the 2025 peak.

Countries have tried it too. In November 2022, El Salvador's president **[Nayib Bukele](/crypto-ogs/nayib-bukele)** announced that the country would buy one Bitcoin every day. The policy has since run into El Salvador's $1.4 billion IMF loan program, approved in February 2025, which restricts new public Bitcoin purchases. Public trackers showed the national reserve at roughly 7,800 BTC in early October 2026, while the IMF says the growth since mid-2025 has come from donations and the consolidation of government wallets rather than fresh buying.

## How to Set Up a DCA Plan

Setting up a plan takes about ten minutes on most major platforms, and the choices you make at the start matter more than any adjustment later:

- **Pick the asset**: most DCA investors stick to Bitcoin, Ethereum or a small basket of large coins, since the strategy depends on the asset still being around in ten years.
- **Pick an amount you can keep paying**: choose a number you could sustain through two years of falling prices without needing the money back. A small plan you never cancel beats a large one you abandon.
- **Pick a frequency**: weekly or monthly is enough. Daily buying smooths the average slightly more, but the difference over several years is small, and it multiplies the number of transactions you need to track.
- **Check the fee on each purchase**: small, frequent buys are where fees hurt most. A hypothetical flat fee of €1 on a €25 purchase is 4 percent gone before the price moves at all, so read the cost on the confirmation screen and buy less often if it is too high.

Most large exchanges now automate the schedule for you. **[Kraken](/exchanges/kraken)** lets you set recurring buys daily, weekly, every two weeks or monthly. **[Coinbase](/exchanges/coinbase)** offers a similar recurring buy option in its app. In Europe, **[Bitpanda](/exchanges/bitpanda)** runs savings plans with a minimum of €10 per purchase. **[Trade Republic](/exchanges/trade-republic)** offers crypto savings plans alongside its stock and ETF plans, which suits anyone who wants their whole investing routine in one place.

Once the plan is running, two housekeeping habits matter. Coins left on an exchange carry that exchange's risks, so many long-term buyers move their balance to a **[hardware wallet](/posts/what-is-a-hardware-wallet)** every few months, in batches to keep network fees down. Every purchase is also its own tax lot with its own cost and date, which in many countries affects how gains are calculated when you sell. A year of weekly buys means 52 lots, so keep the exchange's export files and read our guide to **[crypto taxes](/posts/understanding-crypto-taxes)** before you sell anything.

## Common DCA Mistakes

DCA is simple, but there are a few predictable ways to undermine it:

- **Stopping in a bear market**: cancelling the plan when prices fall throws away the cheap purchases that make the strategy work. Our guide to the **[crypto bear market](/posts/bear-market-in-crypto)** explains why those periods feel worse than they usually turn out to be.
- **Tinkering with the schedule**: pausing when you feel nervous and doubling up when you feel confident turns DCA back into market timing. If you want to buy more when prices fall, do it by formula. Value averaging, described by Michael Edleson in 1988, sets a target growth path for the portfolio and tops it up whenever it falls behind, but it needs more spare cash and more discipline than a plain schedule.
- **Spreading too thin**: splitting $50 a week across ten small tokens adds fees and risk without adding much diversification.
- **Treating it as a guarantee**: DCA lowers the risk of bad timing, not the risk of the market itself. If the asset falls and never recovers, a perfectly executed plan still loses money.

Our price data shows how much the first mistake costs. Take two investors who each started buying $100 of Bitcoin every week on November 1, 2021. One paused in June 2022, when the crash got frightening, and restarted in January 2024 once prices had recovered. The other never touched the plan. At the October 11, 2026 close, the untouched plan had put in $25,800 at an average cost of about $42,700 and was worth about $50,600. The paused plan had put in $17,600 at an average cost of about $66,600 and was worth about $22,100. Skipping the scariest 19 months removed almost every cheap coin the strategy was designed to collect.

## Conclusion: Consistency Beats Timing

Dollar-cost averaging replaces one big decision with many small, automatic ones. You buy a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, the cheap periods pull your average cost down, and you never need to know where the top or the bottom is. Our backtests show both sides of it: buying monthly from the October 2025 peak turned a 33 percent lump-sum loss into a small gain, while in the steady climb of 2023, investing everything on day one came out well ahead.

For most people building a crypto position out of their income, that trade-off is easy to accept. DCA will rarely give you the best possible result, but it reliably protects you from the worst one, and it keeps you buying during the months when your instincts tell you to stop.
