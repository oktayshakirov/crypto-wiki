Jeremy Allaire is the co-founder, chief executive and chairman of Circle, the company behind USDC, and the person who bet that the winning strategy in **[stablecoins](/posts/what-are-stablecoins)** would be to ask regulators for permission rather than forgiveness. That bet took a decade to pay off. It did so decisively in 2025, when Circle listed on the New York Stock Exchange, and again in 2026, when the company launched a blockchain of its own.

He is also, unusually for crypto, a repeat founder from an earlier internet era. Allaire had already built and sold one company and taken another public before Bitcoin existed, which goes some way to explaining why Circle has always behaved more like a financial infrastructure company than a crypto startup.

## From ColdFusion to Online Video

Allaire was born on 13 May 1971 in Philadelphia and raised in Winona, Minnesota, where an early Apple computer his family acquired in 1983 set the direction of his career. He studied political science and philosophy at Macalester College in Saint Paul, graduating in 1993, a background that shows up in how he argues for Circle publicly: more in terms of policy and institutions than protocols.

In 1995 he co-founded Allaire Corporation with his brother JJ. Its product, ColdFusion, was one of the first platforms that made building database-driven web applications practical for ordinary developers. The company went public in January 1999 and was acquired by Macromedia in 2001, where Allaire became chief technology officer and helped shape the Macromedia MX platform.

After a period at the venture firm General Catalyst, he founded Brightcove in 2004, an online video platform that went public in 2012 and where he remains chairman of the board. By the time he turned to crypto he had a track record most of the industry's founders did not: two decades of building internet infrastructure that enterprises actually bought.

## Founding Circle and the Birth of USDC

Allaire co-founded Circle in 2013, early enough that the company's original ambition was consumer **[Bitcoin](/posts/what-is-bitcoin)** payments. That business did not work. What emerged from the pivot was far more consequential.

In 2018 Circle launched USDC, a dollar-pegged token issued initially through the Centre consortium, a joint venture with **[Coinbase](/exchanges/coinbase)** and its chief executive **[Brian Armstrong](/crypto-ogs/brian-armstrong)**. Circle took full control of USDC governance when the consortium was wound down in 2023.

The strategic choice embedded in USDC was transparency as a competitive weapon. Circle held reserves in cash and short-dated US Treasuries, published regular attestations, and pursued money transmission licences rather than operating offshore. For years this looked like an expensive handicap next to **[Paolo Ardoino](/crypto-ogs/paolo-ardoino)** and Tether, whose USDt grew far larger with a far lighter compliance posture. Circle was choosing a smaller market with better paperwork.

USDC became core plumbing regardless, settling on **[Ethereum](/posts/what-is-ethereum)** and a dozen other chains, serving as the default quote asset across **[DeFi](/posts/what-is-defi)**, and underpinning a growing share of **[real world asset tokenization](/posts/real-world-asset-tokenization)**.

## The Silicon Valley Bank Weekend

The strategy nearly broke in March 2023. Circle disclosed that 3.3 billion dollars of USDC reserves, about 8 percent of the total, sat at Silicon Valley Bank when US regulators seized it. With wire windows closed for the weekend, USDC lost its peg and traded as low as roughly 87 cents.

Allaire spent that weekend doing the only thing available to him: publishing, continuously and in public, exactly where the money was and what Circle would do. The company committed corporate funds to cover the shortfall, and after US authorities guaranteed SVB depositors in full, the peg was restored within about 72 hours.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what the episode showed. A fully reserved, fully attested, US-regulated stablecoin still depegged, because the reserves sat in a bank that failed. Transparency is what made the recovery fast, not what prevented the problem. The incident is the strongest available argument both for Circle's disclosure practices and against the idea that any dollar token is truly risk-free.

## Betting the Company on Regulation

The years after SVB vindicated the slower path. Circle obtained an electronic money institution authorisation in France in July 2024, which is why USDC and the euro-denominated EURC remained legally available across Europe when **[MiCA](/posts/what-is-mica)** took effect and Tether, declining to seek the same authorisation, was delisted from European venues.

In the United States, Allaire spent years as one of the industry's most persistent advocates for a federal stablecoin framework, testifying and lobbying for rules that would in practice require competitors to operate the way Circle already did. The **[GENIUS Act](/posts/what-is-the-genius-act)** became law in 2025, finally giving dollar stablecoins a federal statutory basis.

This is the through-line of his career in crypto. Where much of the industry treated regulation as an obstacle to route around, Allaire treated it as the moat itself, on the theory that the institutions holding most of the world's money would only ever use a regulated instrument. For a long stretch this looked like losing. It stopped looking that way in 2025.

## Going Public

Circle listed on the New York Stock Exchange as CRCL on 5 June 2025. The IPO priced at 31 dollars, above its expected range, and the stock closed its first day near 83 dollars, up roughly 168 percent, raising over 1.1 billion dollars and valuing the company above 16 billion. It was one of the strongest debuts of the year and the clearest possible signal that public markets would price a stablecoin issuer as a financial institution rather than a crypto venture.

It also came after an earlier failure. A planned merger with a special purpose acquisition company had been abandoned in late 2022, in the wreckage of that year's collapses. The company that eventually went public did so on conventional terms, with audited financials and a regulatory record, which was the point.

## Arc and the Bigger Bet

In 2026 Allaire moved Circle beyond issuing a token. Arc, a layer-one **[blockchain](/posts/what-is-blockchain)** built for payments and tokenized financial markets, went live on 16 September 2026 with more than 100 founding validators, among them BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, DTCC and Standard Chartered. It uses USDC to pay transaction fees, targets sub-second finality, and launched alongside more than 100 applications. A 222 million dollar presale had closed that May at a 3 billion dollar fully diluted valuation, with a16z crypto, BlackRock, Apollo and Intercontinental Exchange participating. Circle minted 10 billion ARC tokens at genesis and said no public sale was planned.

Allaire called it the most consequential launch in the company's history, more so than USDC itself. The logic is coherent even if the outcome is unproven: issuing a stablecoin makes you a product company dependent on interest income, while running the settlement layer beneath institutional finance makes you infrastructure. It is also a direct answer to the question that has shadowed Circle since the IPO, which is what the business becomes when interest rates fall.

The risks are equally plain. Arc launched with permissioned validators, which invites the obvious criticism that a chain run by a hundred large institutions is a consortium database with extra steps, and the governance questions that come with a possible shift to proof of stake from 2027 are unresolved. Whether Arc becomes financial infrastructure or an expensive detour will not be clear for years.

## Legacy and Influence

Allaire's significance is not technical. He did not invent the stablecoin, and USDC has never been the largest. What he did was demonstrate that the regulated path was commercially viable, at a time when almost every incentive in crypto pointed offshore.

That demonstration shaped policy. USDC became the reference design legislators had in mind when writing stablecoin rules on both sides of the Atlantic, which means the GENIUS Act and MiCA's e-money token regime both encode assumptions drawn from how Circle already operated. Competitors now comply with a framework built substantially around one company's practices, and European exchanges including **[Bitvavo](/exchanges/bitvavo)** list USDC precisely because of that compliance.

The open question is whether regulatory alignment is a durable advantage or a temporary one. As rules standardise, every compliant issuer meets the same bar, and banks and **[central bank digital currencies](/posts/central-bank-digital-currencies)** may eventually compete for the same role with deeper balance sheets. Arc is Allaire's answer to that risk, and the most consequential unfinished part of his record.
