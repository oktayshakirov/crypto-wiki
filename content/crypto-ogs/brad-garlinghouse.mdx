Brad Garlinghouse is an American technology executive who has been chief executive of Ripple since 2016, and he is the person most responsible for turning a cryptocurrency company into an enterprise payments business that argued with the US government and largely won. He came to crypto as a career operator rather than a cryptographer, and that background explains both his approach and the criticism of it.

## The Peanut Butter Manifesto Years

Garlinghouse was a known quantity in Silicon Valley long before XRP.

As a senior vice president at Yahoo he wrote a 2006 internal memo that leaked and became genuinely famous. Its argument was that Yahoo was spreading its resources like peanut butter, a thin layer over everything and a meaningful investment in nothing, and that the company needed to pick a small number of things and win them. He was right, Yahoo did not listen, and the memo became a standard case study in strategic focus.

He went on to senior roles at AOL and then the chief executive position at Hightail, the file-sharing company formerly called YouSendIt. He joined Ripple in 2015 as chief operating officer and became CEO the following year.

None of that is a crypto background, and it is the most useful thing to know about him. He arrived with an operator's instincts: pick a customer, sell them something specific, ignore everything else. Ripple's strategy has been an application of the Peanut Butter Manifesto to an industry that mostly does the opposite.

## What Ripple Actually Sells

The persistent confusion about Ripple is the relationship between the company, the XRP Ledger and the XRP token, and Garlinghouse has spent a decade trying to separate them in public.

Ripple is a private company selling software to financial institutions. Its products handle cross-border payments, where the legacy system requires banks to pre-fund accounts in destination countries, tying up capital indefinitely. Ripple's On-Demand Liquidity product uses XRP as a bridge asset so that value moves without those pre-funded positions, which is a narrow and genuinely real problem in correspondent banking rather than a general claim about **[blockchain](/posts/what-is-blockchain)** replacing finance.

The XRP Ledger is separate open-source infrastructure that runs on a consensus protocol without mining, which makes it fast and cheap relative to **[Bitcoin](/posts/what-is-bitcoin)** and gives it a much smaller energy footprint, a point Garlinghouse raises often in debates about **[crypto and the environment](/posts/crypto-and-the-environment)**.

The company has also pushed into stablecoins and custody. RLUSD, its dollar stablecoin, launched in December 2024 and is now used as collateral across its prime brokerage products.

## Four and a Half Years Against the SEC

In December 2020 the SEC sued Ripple, Garlinghouse and co-founder Chris Larsen, alleging they had conducted an unregistered securities offering worth more than a billion dollars through XRP sales. US exchanges delisted XRP within weeks. It became the defining legal fight of the era, and Garlinghouse chose to contest it rather than settle, which almost nobody else in his position did.

The sequence matters, because it is frequently reported wrongly.

In July 2023 Judge Analisa Torres issued a split summary judgment. Programmatic sales of XRP on public exchanges were not, in themselves, securities offerings, because buyers on an order book had no way of knowing whether they were buying from Ripple. Certain institutional sales, where Ripple sold directly under contract, did satisfy the Howey test. Exchanges relisted XRP on the back of the retail half of that ruling.

In October 2023 the SEC dismissed its claims against Garlinghouse and Larsen personally, with prejudice, meaning they cannot be refiled. Ripple's chief legal officer called it a surrender rather than a settlement.

In August 2024 Torres ordered Ripple to pay a 125 million dollar civil penalty and imposed a permanent injunction against future violations, far below the roughly two billion the SEC had sought. Garlinghouse announced on March 19, 2025 that the SEC was abandoning its appeal, and Ripple dropped its cross-appeal on June 27, 2025.

One correction worth making, because it circulates constantly: the penalty was not reduced. Both parties jointly asked the court for an indicative ruling that would have dissolved the injunction and cut the penalty by 60 percent to 50 million dollars. Torres denied that motion on June 26, 2025, finding they had not come close to showing the exceptional circumstances needed to vacate a final judgment. The 125 million dollar penalty and the injunction on institutional sales both stand.

## Rebuilding as an Institutional Firm

With the litigation resolved, Garlinghouse spent Ripple's balance sheet aggressively, and the pattern is coherent: buy the licences and the plumbing that let a crypto company sell to regulated institutions.

Ripple acquired Metaco, the Swiss institutional custody provider used by banks, for 250 million dollars in 2023. It bought Standard Custody and Trust Company, whose limited purpose trust charter and money transmitter licences added directly to Ripple's regulatory portfolio. In April 2025 it agreed to acquire Hidden Road, one of the fastest-growing non-bank prime brokers, for 1.25 billion dollars, completing the deal and renaming it Ripple Prime in October 2025. Reported totals for the acquisition programme run to roughly three billion dollars.

This is the Peanut Butter Manifesto applied again. Rather than building a retail exchange to compete with **[Coinbase](/exchanges/coinbase)** or **[Kraken](/exchanges/kraken)**, Ripple assembled custody, prime brokerage and a stablecoin aimed at institutions that need licensed counterparties. Garlinghouse publicly quashed IPO speculation in March 2025.

## The Centralization Argument He Cannot Shake

The criticism that has followed Garlinghouse throughout is that XRP is not meaningfully decentralised, and it has substance.

Ripple has historically held a very large share of XRP supply, released from escrow on a schedule the company controls, which gives it influence over the asset's float that no Bitcoin holder has over Bitcoin. The XRP Ledger's consensus model relies on a list of trusted validators rather than open mining, a design that is faster and cheaper but places trust in a known set of participants.

Garlinghouse's answers are that validator diversity has increased substantially, that Ripple publishes regular disclosures on its holdings and sales, that the escrow schedule is transparent and predictable, and that the company cannot unilaterally change ledger rules. He has argued the point by noting that Ripple could not prevent or reverse a hack of XRP it did not hold, and has said publicly that he is not an XRP maximalist and that customer needs come before token advocacy.

The honest position is that the objection is real and partly mitigated rather than refuted. Anyone evaluating XRP should understand that its supply dynamics and validator model differ fundamentally from **[Ethereum](/posts/what-is-ethereum)** or from a **[proof-of-work](/posts/what-is-proof-of-work)** chain, and price the difference themselves rather than accepting either side's framing.

## Where Garlinghouse Sits Now

Garlinghouse remains Ripple's chief executive as of September 2026, still the company's principal public voice, and he has broadened his commentary beyond XRP, arguing that multiple chains including Solana can succeed simultaneously.

His significance is not technical. He did not design a protocol or invent a mechanism. What he did was refuse to settle a securities case that most of the industry assumed was unwinnable, spend four and a half years and a nine-figure legal budget on it, and establish a precedent that a token traded on an exchange is not automatically a security in the hands of its retail buyer. That ruling reshaped how US firms discuss token distribution, and it happened because one company had the resources and the stubbornness to litigate.

He is also a reminder that enterprise adoption arguments are easy to make and hard to verify. Ripple has announced a great many partnerships over a decade, and the volume actually settling through its products has consistently been harder to pin down than the announcements suggest. On a page about a living executive that tension is worth stating plainly: the legal victory is a matter of public record, and the scale of the payments business is largely a matter of company disclosure. Treat the two differently, and apply the same scepticism you would to any claim about **[understanding crypto exchanges](/posts/understanding-crypto-exchanges)** and the institutions built around them.
