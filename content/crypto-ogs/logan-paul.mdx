## **Logan Paul: The Influencer Whose Crypto Bets Became a Cautionary Tale**

Logan Paul is an American YouTuber, podcaster and WWE wrestler whose crypto ventures, above all the failed CryptoZoo NFT game, made him the best-known example of a celebrity crypto promotion gone wrong. Between 2021 and 2022 he launched an NFT game, promoted several **[memecoins](/posts/what-are-memecoins)**, sold NFTs of his own and co-founded a platform for fractional ownership of collectibles. None of them ended the way he promised.

What followed was four years of investigations, refunds, lawsuits and counter-lawsuits. It also produced one of the most closely watched legal questions in the influencer economy: whether a celebrity who promotes a crypto project can be held responsible when it collapses.

> "A really fun game that makes you money." - Logan Paul, describing CryptoZoo on his Impaulsive podcast in August 2021

## **Early Life and Rise to Fame**

Logan Alexander Paul was born on 1 April 1995 in Westlake, Ohio, a suburb of Cleveland. He was a competitive wrestler at Westlake High School and went on to study industrial engineering at Ohio University. He left in 2014 to make videos full time.

He first built an audience on Vine, the short-video app, and then on YouTube, where his daily vlogs drew millions of subscribers. In November 2018 he launched the Impaulsive podcast, which became his main platform for talking to his fans. Over the following years he fought professional boxing exhibitions, including one against Floyd Mayweather in June 2021. He signed with WWE in 2022 and later won its United States Championship. In 2022 he co-founded the Prime drinks brand with the British YouTuber KSI, by far his most commercially successful venture.

That reach, tens of millions of mostly young followers across several platforms, is what made his move into crypto matter. When Paul promoted a token or an NFT, the audience it reached was larger than most crypto projects could ever buy.

## **Entry into Crypto During the 2021 Mania**

Paul arrived in crypto at the peak of the 2021 bull market, when NFTs, memecoins and play-to-earn games were all being sold as ways for ordinary people to get rich. He moved to Puerto Rico that year, a destination that had become popular with crypto investors because of its tax incentives, and began talking about digital assets regularly on his podcast and in his paid fan community, the Maverick Club.

In July 2021 he also made his biggest single collectibles purchase: a PSA 10 graded Pikachu Illustrator, one of the rarest Pokemon cards in existence, for about 5.275 million dollars. The card would later become part of his crypto story through Liquid Marketplace.

## **Elongate and Dink Doink: The Memecoin Promotions**

Paul's first documented crypto promotions were memecoins. In May 2021 he posted a video to the Maverick Club about Elongate, a token themed on **[Elon Musk](/crypto-ogs/elon-musk)**, saying "Elongate made me rich." Its price spiked and then collapsed within hours, the pattern familiar from countless low-liquidity tokens.

In June 2021 he promoted Dink Doink, a memecoin built around a cartoon character, telling his followers on his podcast and on Twitter that he was all in on it. He did not say at the time that he had helped create the project. According to a TIME investigation published in February 2023, the project was led by his roommate, Paul received about 2.5 percent of the initial token supply at launch, and he later acknowledged his involvement to the New York Times. TIME also reported that a wallet which bought a large amount of DINK minutes after launch sent 36.5 ETH, then worth more than 100,000 dollars, to Paul's public wallet in August 2021. Paul's lawyers said the transfer was unrelated to Dink Doink. He later called the token "the project from hell."

In November 2024 a BBC investigation revisited these promotions. It reported that anonymous wallets appeared to buy several tokens shortly before Paul promoted them and to sell after his posts drove prices up, with one reportedly making around 120,000 dollars. The BBC said Paul repeatedly declined interview requests, and he did not publicly answer the allegations at the time.

## **CryptoZoo: The Game That Never Shipped**

CryptoZoo was Paul's own project and by far his largest. Announced in mid-2021 and partly inspired by Pokemon, it was pitched as a play-to-earn game in the style of **[GameFi](/posts/what-is-gamefi)**. Players would buy NFT eggs, mostly with a new project token called ZOO, hatch them into animals, then breed those animals into rarer hybrids that could be sold. Paul said he had put about 1 million dollars of his own money into development.

He promoted it heavily. In an October 2021 interview with Forbes he called CryptoZoo potentially more relatable than Pokemon and said kids would care about the blockchain because of his project.

The launch on 3 September 2021 went well at first: the first 10,000 Base Eggs sold out within minutes. But the promised game never appeared in playable form, and the ZOO token sank. Buyers who had spent hundreds or thousands of dollars were left holding NFTs of stock-image animals with no game to use them in. Some had spent far more. TIME reported on one 20-year-old in the UK who put in around 30,000 pounds of his own money plus a 50,000 pound loan from his father, almost all of it lost.

Meanwhile Paul's public involvement faded. By April 2022 he was describing his role in CryptoZoo as "super minimal." Many buyers who had followed his promotion into the project saw it as a textbook **[rug pull](/posts/what-is-a-rug-pull)**, even if no one ran off with a treasury in a single transaction.

## **The Coffeezilla Investigation and the Apology**

The collapse might have stayed a niche grievance if not for Stephen Findeisen, the YouTuber known as Coffeezilla. In December 2022 he released a three-part series on CryptoZoo, based on interviews with buyers and with people involved in the project. It alleged that the founders had misled investors and each other, that the game had never worked, and that Paul had walked away from the people who bought in on his word.

Paul's first response, in early January 2023, was combative. He posted a video disputing the allegations and threatening legal action against Coffeezilla, then deleted it. Days later he changed course. He apologised, said he had trusted the wrong people and had hired "conmen" and "felons" to build the project, and pledged to refund holders at least 1.3 million dollars, give up his stake and finish the game.

> "To say I am disappointed in how this was handled internally is an understatement." - Logan Paul, January 2023

The finished game never arrived. The refund took another year.

## **The Buyback and the Lawsuits**

On 2 February 2023 a group of CryptoZoo buyers filed a proposed class action, Holland v. CryptoZoo, in the federal court for the Western District of Texas. The suit named Paul and five others connected to the project, and alleged fraud, negligence and violations of Texas consumer protection law.

On 4 January 2024 Paul announced a buyback. He said he would personally commit more than 2.3 million dollars to buy Base Eggs and Base Animals from holders at 0.1 ETH each, with claims open until 8 February 2024. The terms drew immediate criticism: anyone accepting the money had to waive all claims against him, Paul alone decided which NFTs were eligible, hybrid animals were excluded, and the ZOO token that many buyers had used was not covered at all. The same week, he filed cross-claims against two of his CryptoZoo co-defendants, Eduardo Ibanez and Jake Greenbaum, blaming them for the project's failure.

The class action moved slowly. On 29 October 2025 Judge Alan Albright adopted a magistrate judge's recommendation and dismissed all 27 claims against Paul. The court found that statements such as a "fun game that makes you money" were non-actionable promotional puffery, and that the plaintiffs had not shown he knew the project would fail. Paul's lawyer, Jeffrey Neiman, said the judge had found that "no reasonable juror could find Logan's statements misleading or fraudulent."

It was not the end, however. Twenty-six of the claims were dismissed with leave to amend, and the plaintiffs filed a new complaint. In February 2026 Paul asked the court to dismiss that one too, arguing it still did not show he could be held liable for CryptoZoo's conduct. No ruling on that motion had been publicly reported as of October 2026.

Paul also went on offence. On 27 June 2024 he sued Coffeezilla for defamation in federal court in San Antonio, a venue where Texas anti-SLAPP protections were not available to Findeisen. In March 2025 a magistrate judge recommended denying Findeisen's motion to dismiss, finding that calling the project a scam could be read as a factual claim rather than opinion. A jury trial was set for May 2026. Instead, the two sides settled out of court in July 2026 and the case was dismissed with prejudice. No terms were made public.

## **Liquid Marketplace and the Record Pokemon Card**

Paul's other major crypto-adjacent business was Liquid Marketplace, a platform for buying fractions of high-value physical collectibles such as Pokemon and sports cards, a consumer version of **[real world asset tokenization](/posts/real-world-asset-tokenization)**. It announced an 8 million dollar raise in April 2022, with Paul as a co-founder and chief strategy officer.

The Pikachu Illustrator was its showcase listing. Paul offered up to 51 percent of the card on the platform in 2022, but only 5.4 percent sold, for about 270,000 dollars. In May 2024 he bought those fractions back at the same price under the platform's terms. Liquid Marketplace later went offline; Paul said this happened for reasons outside his control and that he personally paid to restore the site so users could withdraw their funds.

In June 2024 the Ontario Securities Commission brought fraud allegations against Liquid Marketplace and three of its executives, including CEO Ryan Bahadori, alleging that about 3 million dollars of more than 10 million raised from investors had been misappropriated. Paul was not named as a respondent, and the allegations had not been proven at the time they were filed.

The card itself turned out to be his best investment by far. On 16 February 2026 it sold at a Goldin auction for 16,492,000 dollars to A.J. Scaramucci, son of the financier Anthony Scaramucci. Guinness World Records certified it as the most expensive trading card ever sold at auction, about three times what Paul paid in 2021.

## **NFTs and Other Ventures**

Paul was an active NFT seller during the boom. In 2022 he launched 99 Originals, a series of Polaroid photographs auctioned as NFTs one at a time over 99 days, which he had described as the craziest thing he had ever done. Like most celebrity collections of that era, interest faded quickly once the NFT market turned, a trend that left many buyers asking whether **[NFTs are dead](/posts/are-nfts-dead)**. Other celebrities who embraced NFTs in the same period, such as **[Snoop Dogg](/crypto-ogs/snoop-dogg)**, kept building in the space; Paul largely stopped talking about crypto after CryptoZoo's collapse.

## **Legacy and Influence**

Logan Paul is not a crypto builder in the way most figures on this site are. He wrote no protocol and ran no exchange. His importance comes from reach: for millions of young fans, his promotions were their first contact with crypto, and for many of them that contact ended in a loss.

The CryptoZoo case also became a test of how far promotional language can go before it becomes fraud. So far the courts have treated his words as puffery, and his legal record is that of a man who has not been found liable for anything. His record with the buyers who trusted him is harder to defend, and his story remains the standard reference whenever regulators and journalists discuss celebrity endorsements. For readers, the lesson is the same one at the heart of **[how to avoid crypto scams](/posts/how-to-avoid-crypto-scams)**: an endorsement says nothing about whether a project works, so **[do your own research](/posts/do-your-own-research)** before you buy anything a famous person tells you to.
