## **Jeff Yan: The Quant Who Built Hyperliquid Without Investors**

Jeff Yan is an American quantitative trader and the co-founder and CEO of Hyperliquid Labs, the small team behind **[Hyperliquid](/exchanges/hyperliquid)**, the dominant on-chain exchange for perpetual futures. A former Hudson River Trading engineer and International Physics Olympiad gold medallist, he built the platform with his own trading profits, refused venture capital, and gave 31 percent of its token to users rather than to investors.

That combination made him one of the most unusual founders of the 2020s crypto cycle. As of late 2025, Hyperliquid Labs had just 11 core contributors, yet the exchange processed around 308 billion dollars of volume in October 2025 alone, according to DefiLlama data cited by CoinDesk. Yan rarely gives interviews, keeps a deliberately low profile, and posts on X under the handle chameleon_jeff, a nod to the trading firm he ran before Hyperliquid existed.

> "Our philosophy is simple: create a product that users genuinely like and are willing to use." - Jeff Yan, on what drove Hyperliquid's growth

## **Early Life and Education**

Yan grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area, the son of Chinese immigrant parents. He was a competition kid in the most literal sense: he trained in mathematics and physics olympiads through high school and, in 2013, won a gold medal at the International Physics Olympiad in Copenhagen.

He went on to Harvard University, where he studied mathematics and computer science. Profiles of him describe internships across engineering and quantitative finance during those years, and the pattern was already clear. He was drawn to problems where speed, probability and system design meet, which is a fair description of what a modern derivatives exchange is.

## **Hudson River Trading and a First Crypto Attempt**

After Harvard, Yan joined Hudson River Trading, one of the largest high-frequency trading firms in the United States. HRT's software places and cancels orders in fractions of a second, and the job gave him a working education in market microstructure: how order books behave, how market makers price risk, and how small engineering decisions turn into large differences in execution.

He did not stay long. According to a 2026 profile, he left after less than a year because he questioned how much value he was adding. In 2018 he co-founded a blockchain-based prediction market, one of the first attempts to put betting on real-world events on a public ledger. It never found an audience and was shut down. The idea was early, years before **[prediction markets](/posts/what-are-prediction-markets)** became a mainstream product, and the failure taught him something he would later repeat often: build what users actually want, not what sounds clever.

## **Chameleon Trading and the FTX Turning Point**

Around 2020, Yan moved to Puerto Rico and started Chameleon Trading, a crypto market-making firm, with roughly 10,000 dollars of starting capital according to profiles of him. The firm traded on centralized venues such as Coinbase and grew quickly through the 2020 and 2021 bull run. By his late twenties he was financially independent.

The market-making years gave him something more valuable than capital: an inside view of how opaque centralized exchanges really were. When FTX collapsed in November 2022 and customer funds turned out to have been used by Alameda Research, the trading firm controlled by **[Sam Bankman-Fried](/crypto-ogs/sam-bankman)**, Yan saw the failure as a design flaw rather than bad luck. Users had no way to verify what an exchange did with their money.

> "The FTX thing solidified my conviction that it was the right time to build this thing." - Jeff Yan, speaking to Fortune in January 2026

He wound down Chameleon and turned the team's attention to a fully on-chain exchange.

## **Building Hyperliquid Without Investors**

Hyperliquid opened to traders in early 2023, built by Yan alongside a pseudonymous co-founder known as iliensinc. Its pitch was simple to state and hard to execute: a **[decentralized exchange](/posts/what-is-a-dex)** that feels like a professional centralized one. Instead of an automated market maker, it runs a central limit order book on its own layer-1 blockchain, with every order, cancellation and fill recorded on-chain and collateral held in users' own wallets.

The product it specialised in was **[perpetual futures](/posts/what-are-perpetual-futures)**, the leveraged contracts with no expiry date that **[Arthur Hayes](/crypto-ogs/arthur-hayes)** popularised at BitMEX in 2016 and that now dominate crypto trading volume. Yan's bet was that perps did not need a custodian at all if the chain underneath was fast enough.

The funding model was as deliberate as the technology. Yan paid for development with Chameleon's profits and, according to profiles published in 2026, turned down a 2024 offer that would have valued the project at about 1 billion dollars. In a manifesto published on 28 January 2024, Hyperliquid spelled out the principle in four lines: "No investors. No paid market makers. No fees to the dev team. No insiders."

> "A really important principle is to sort of not have insiders." - Jeff Yan, explaining to Fortune why he refused venture money

## **The HYPE Airdrop**

On 29 November 2024, that principle became concrete. Hyperliquid launched its HYPE token through a genesis distribution of 310 million tokens, 31 percent of the 1 billion supply, to early users who had earned points by trading on the platform. Venture funds received nothing, because there were none. A further 38.88 percent was reserved for future community rewards, 23.8 percent for current and future core contributors, and 6 percent for the Hyper Foundation.

It was one of the largest **[crypto airdrops](/posts/understanding-crypto-airdrops)** in history, and it rewrote expectations for how a token launch could work. Most projects of the era launched with large insider allocations and long unlock schedules that hung over the price. HYPE had no such overhang, and roughly 97 percent of protocol fees were directed to an Assistance Fund that buys HYPE on the open market. By mid-2025 the token's market capitalisation was near 20 billion dollars, according to CoinDesk.

Yan himself has never disclosed how much HYPE he holds. Estimates of his net worth circulate widely, but they are guesses built on assumptions about his share of the contributor pool, which is meant for the whole team.

## **Stress Tests: JELLY and 10 October 2025**

Running a leveraged exchange means living through the moments when leverage breaks. Hyperliquid's first major test came in March 2025, when a trader used a thin memecoin market called JELLYJELLY to attack the protocol's own liquidity vault. Validators voted to delist the market and settle positions, which prevented losses but drew sharp criticism that a supposedly decentralized protocol had overridden a live market.

The far bigger test came on 10 October 2025. A tariff announcement from **[Donald Trump](/crypto-ogs/donald-trump)** triggered the largest liquidation cascade in crypto history: more than 19 billion dollars of leveraged positions were wiped out across the market, and more than 10 billion dollars of that happened on Hyperliquid, according to CoinGlass data cited by Fortune. For the first time in more than two years of operation, the exchange triggered cross-margin auto-deleveraging, closing some profitable positions to keep the system solvent. Anyone unfamiliar with how **[crypto liquidations](/posts/what-is-a-crypto-liquidation)** chain together got a brutal lesson that night.

> "I'm just looking at it and praying that it's good." - Jeff Yan, describing watching Hyperliquid's systems during the 10 October 2025 crash

The exchange stayed online and solvent through the crash. Critics, including risk researchers, argued that its deleveraging design had cost traders more than necessary, and others claimed it favoured protocol revenue. Yan dismissed the revenue claims as FUD and argued that centralized exchanges underreport their own liquidations, pointing to documentation showing that **[Binance](/exchanges/binance)** logs only one liquidation per second even when thousands occur.

## **From Exchange to Market Infrastructure**

The next phase of Yan's plan was to stop being just an exchange. On 13 October 2025, Hyperliquid switched on HIP-3, which lets outside builders deploy their own perpetual markets on its order book by staking HYPE. Third-party venues quickly used it to list markets the core team never would, including perps on **[tokenized stocks](/posts/what-are-tokenized-stocks)** and pre-IPO companies such as SpaceX.

Traditional finance began paying attention. At an investor conference in May 2026, Intercontinental Exchange founder Jeff Sprecher, whose company owns the New York Stock Exchange, called Hyperliquid "bigger than Nasdaq" and said ICE had held several talks with the team. In September 2026, Payward, the parent company of **[Kraken](/exchanges/kraken)**, announced plans to offer permissioned Hyperliquid markets to US clients through its CFTC-licensed derivatives business, pending regulatory approval.

Yan framed this as the goal all along. In an interview with Bloomberg on 15 October 2026, he described Hyperliquid as infrastructure that existing market operators could build on rather than replace.

> "We're not trying to displace anything." - Jeff Yan, Bloomberg interview, 15 October 2026

## **Washington, Singapore and Scrutiny**

Growth brought regulators to the door. On 18 February 2026, the Hyperliquid Policy Center opened in Washington as an independent nonprofit led by crypto lawyer Jake Chervinsky, funded with 1 million HYPE from the Hyper Foundation, worth roughly 29 million dollars at launch according to Decrypt. Yan said on X that the ecosystem had lacked a unified voice in policy debates.

On 26 June 2026, the Monetary Authority of Singapore added Hyperliquid to its Investor Alert List, a consumer warning that an entity is not licensed there. MAS described it as a warning rather than an enforcement action, and Hyperliquid responded that it had never claimed a Singapore licence. In August 2026, President Trump said officials were working to bring the platform onshore in the United States, where it is still closed to US customers.

The most serious scrutiny arrived on 29 September 2026. House Oversight Committee Chairman James Comer sent Yan a records request as part of an inquiry into insider trading on trading and prediction market platforms. The letter cited reports that a leveraged short worth around 1.1 billion dollars had been opened on Hyperliquid minutes before the October 2025 tariff announcement, and asked about identity checks and suspicious-trade monitoring, with a 13 October deadline. A records request is not a subpoena or an allegation against Yan himself, but it put Hyperliquid at the center of a federal inquiry for the first time.

His public answer came in the Bloomberg interview two weeks later. He argued that US oversight should fall on the firms that deploy and run markets on Hyperliquid, not on the blockchain underneath them, because "the deployers are the ones that exercise these controls."

## **Philosophy and Public Persona**

Yan's views on crypto are less ideological than those of many earlier pioneers, but they are consistent. He believes that markets work better when their rules are public and enforced by code, that a token should belong to the people who use the product, and that venture money changes what a project becomes. In one podcast interview he compared it directly to **[Bitcoin](/posts/what-is-bitcoin)**, saying that if Bitcoin had raised venture rounds, he did not think it would be Bitcoin.

His management style is just as distinctive. He has kept the team tiny on purpose, arguing that a bad hire is worse than no hire, and announced in late 2025 that Hyperliquid Labs would grow from 11 to only 14 people. The protocol generated more than 616 million dollars in annualised revenue as of January 2026, according to Fortune, an extraordinary figure for a team that size.

Fame has not come easily to him. After being mobbed by fans at a conference in South Korea, he told Fortune that "this sort of celebrity is foreign to me," and profiles report that he now travels with security.

## **Legacy and Influence**

Jeff Yan's lasting contribution is proof that a decentralized exchange can compete with centralized giants on speed and depth while letting users keep custody of their funds. Rivals such as **[Aster](/exchanges/aster)** launched to chase the market he defined, and Wall Street exchanges now study his order book rather than dismiss it. Just as important is the funding model: no investors, a token distributed to users, and a team small enough to fit in one room. Hyperliquid has become the reference point in any debate over whether crypto projects need venture capital at all.

His record also carries open questions. The JELLY intervention and the 10 October 2025 deleveraging showed that "decentralized" still includes moments of human judgement, and the congressional and Singapore scrutiny of 2026 shows that regulators are no longer willing to treat Hyperliquid as an experiment at the edge of the market. Whether Yan can bring the platform into regulated markets without giving up the principles that made it work will decide whether he is remembered as the founder of one great exchange or as the architect of a new kind of **[DeFi](/posts/what-is-defi)** market infrastructure.
